# Summary

Many engineering materials are heterogeneous at a small enough scale. In particle-reinforced composites, porous materials and polymer blends, particles or voids of one phase are embedded in a matrix of another, and their arrangement controls the macroscopic behaviour of the material. Computational homogenization predicts this behaviour by solving a boundary value problem on a representative volume element (RVE), a sample of the microstructure that represents the material in an average sense [@HILL1963357].
GMMD (Geometric Methods for Microstructure Design) is an open-source Python package that generates periodic RVEs of particle-reinforced materials, meshes them for finite element and FFT-based solvers, and measures their spatial statistics.
The particles are disks, ellipses and squares in 2D, and spheres, ellipsoids, cylinders and long fibres in 3D. Their sizes, shapes and orientations follow statistical distributions given in a single YAML input file. GMMD places them without overlap by a time-driven molecular dynamics (MD) method [@VILACHA2021104069; @FERREIRA2022104068] or by random sequential adsorption (RSA).

# Statement of need

An RVE of a particle-reinforced material must reach the volume fraction of the real material, follow its measured distributions of particle size, shape and orientation, and be periodic, so that periodic boundary conditions apply. It must also be cheap enough to generate many times: to check that the RVE is large enough, to average over realizations, and to build the datasets of data-driven material design [@BESSA2017320].
RSA, which places particles one at a time at random positions, is fast, but it stops at a saturation limit of about 55 % for identical disks and 38 % for identical spheres [@Torquato2002, p. 88]. Many composites exceed these fractions.
In the MD method of GMMD, the particles move and repel each other until no overlap remains, with an adaptive time step and isokinetic thermostats that control their velocities [@VILACHA2021104069]. This method reaches 80 % for identical disks and 60 % for identical spheres, and the statistics of its RVEs agree with those measured on a carbon-fibre-reinforced metal composite and on a PC/ABS polymer blend [@FERREIRA2022104068]. GMMD also offers RSA, for volume fractions below its saturation limit.

GMMD is meant for researchers in computational mechanics and materials design who need many RVEs and want one tool from the description of a microstructure to its mesh. The methods are purely geometric: they do not simulate how the microstructure forms.
Each run writes the particles to a human-readable file, `mic.json`. From this file, GMMD builds periodic Gmsh meshes [@Geuzaine2009] that conform to the particle surfaces, translated for the finite element codes LINKS, CRATE, Abaqus and FEniCS, and voxel grids for FFT-based solvers. The same file feeds the analysis tools: the two-point correlation function, Ripley's K function, nearest-neighbour distances, and Voronoi metrics based on Minkowski tensors [@SchroderTurk2011].
GMMD also plots the final configuration (PDF in 2D, VTK in 2D and 3D) and animates 2D generations, and the same seed gives the same microstructure on every run. \autoref{fig:example} shows an RVE of spheres and ellipsoids.

# State of the field

GMMD is part of an ecosystem of open source microstructure generators.
Neper [@Neper2011; @Neper2018], for instance, generates polycrystalline microstructures via Laguerre tessellations. The seed positions and weights for tessellation are adjusted via an optimization algorithm until the resulting tessellation converges to the desired morphology.
Kanapy [@Kanapy2019], also tailored for polycrystalline microstructures, generates microstructures via collision driven particle dynamics.
Albeit not primarily designed for microstructure generation, PoreSpy [@gostick2019porespy] is tailored for porous materials.
Lastly, MicroStructPy [@hart2020microstructpy] generates microstructures via packing geometries (with controlled overlap), approximating them by multi-circles and then using them as seeds to tessellate the domain. It is suitable for polycrystalline materials while also handling porous and particle reinforced materials.

GMMD is unique in that it generates microstructures for particle reinforced materials, it supports a broad library of particle shapes and its architecture allows for easy integration of new ones and can use time-driven molecular dynamics or random sequential adsorption as the generation method, not making use of tesselation nor optimization.

# AI usage disclosure

The first version of GMMD was written in 2020 and 2021 without generative AI tools.
Since 2026, generative AI tools have been used in its development:

- Claude Code (Anthropic), with the models Claude Opus 4.6, Claude Opus 5, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5, was used to write and refactor code, to write tests, to review code, and to draft documentation (the README, the Sphinx documentation and the reference input file). It was also used to copy-edit this paper.
- GitHub Copilot was used for code completion in the editor and, through Copilot Autofix, to propose fixes to code scanning alerts.

The human authors reviewed, edited and validated all AI-assisted output, and made the core design decisions.
A commit made with an AI assistant names the tool and model in an `Assisted-by:` trailer (`Co-authored-by:` in earlier commits); code completion in the editor is not marked.
